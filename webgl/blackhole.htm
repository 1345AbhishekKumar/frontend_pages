CHAPTER I — FORMATION · R = 27 rs

The Standoff

A star is a negotiation. Gravity pulls everything inward; pressure pushes back. For millions of years the ledger balances, and the star shines. The whole treaty is one line:

\dfrac{dP}{dr} = -\dfrac{G\,M(r)\,\rho}{r^{2}}
Hydrostatic equilibrium — the truce every star makes with gravity

When the fuel runs out, the ledger breaks. The core contracts — and what stops it next is not chemistry, but quantum mechanics.

CHAPTER I — FORMATION · R = 24.5 rs

The Limit

Electrons refuse to be stacked into the same state. That refusal — degeneracy pressure — can hold a dead core forever. But only up to a weight:

M_{\mathrm{Ch}} \simeq \dfrac{1}{m_{p}^{2}}\Big(\dfrac{\hbar c}{G}\Big)^{3/2} \approx 1.44\,M_{\odot}
Chandrasekhar limit — beyond it, no force in the universe can win

Past this mass the electrons are crushed into protons. A neutron core gets one last line of defense — but in Einstein's theory, pressure itself gravitates. The harder matter fights, the heavier it becomes:

\dfrac{dP}{dr} = -\dfrac{G\,\big(\rho + P/c^{2}\big)\big(m + 4\pi r^{3}P/c^{2}\big)}{r^{2}\big(1 - 2Gm/rc^{2}\big)}
Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff — above ~3 M☉, no solution exists

CHAPTER I — FORMATION · R = 22 rs

The Fall

The core collapses at a quarter the speed of light. In under a second, ten thousand kilometres of dying star fall through a single radius — the one where escape demands the speed of light:

r_{s} = \dfrac{2GM}{c^{2}} \approx 2.95\ \mathrm{km}\,\big(M/M_{\odot}\big)
The Schwarzschild radius — 29.5 km for 10 M☉

Oppenheimer and Snyder proved what happens next: seen from outside, the star dims and freezes at its horizon, forever. Seen from the star, it simply keeps falling. What remains is not an object. It is a region where the future points inward. It is in front of you now — 10 M☉, 29.5 km across.

CHAPTER II — GEOMETRY · R = 19.5 rs

The Geometry

Karl Schwarzschild found this solution in December 1915, computing by hand while serving on the Eastern Front. He would be dead within six months. His equation outlived him by, so far, forever:

ds^{2} = -\Big(1-\dfrac{r_{s}}{r}\Big)c^{2}dt^{2} + \Big(1-\dfrac{r_{s}}{r}\Big)^{-1}dr^{2} + r^{2}d\Omega^{2}
The Schwarzschild metric — time slows, space stretches, geometry is the gravity

Read it carefully: it is not a force. Near the mass, clocks tick slower and radial distance stretches. Orbits, light, and falling are just the straightest possible lines through a geometry that has itself curved.

CHAPTER II — GEOMETRY · R = 17.5 rs

Straight Lines

Nothing here is pulled. Light and matter simply follow the straightest available path through curved spacetime:

\dfrac{d^{2}x^{\mu}}{d\lambda^{2}} + \Gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\,\dfrac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda}\dfrac{dx^{\beta}}{d\lambda} = 0
Geodesics — the free-fall equation of the universe

For photons, the paths have a closed form — and every pixel of the scene behind these words is integrating it, per frame, from your eye outward:

\dfrac{d^{2}u}{d\phi^{2}} + u = \tfrac{3}{2}\,r_{s}\,u^{2} \qquad u \equiv 1/r
Photon orbits — integrated per pixel, in this page, right now

Three Radii

Schwarzschild's geometry carves sharp thresholds out of empty space:

r_{\mathrm{ph}} = \dfrac{3GM}{c^{2}} = \tfrac{3}{2}r_{s} \qquad\quad r_{\mathrm{ISCO}} = \dfrac{6GM}{c^{2}} = 3r_{s}
Light orbits at 1.5 rs; matter holds orbits only above 3

At 1.5 rs photons can circle the hole — the bright ring hugging the shadow in front of you. At 3 rs, stable orbits end: below it, matter stops circling and starts falling. At 1 rs, the future itself turns inward.

CHAPTER III — THE APPROACH · R = 12 rs

The Approach

The first thing to change is not the dark — it is the light. Starlight from behind the hole bends around it and reaches you from impossible angles. The disk seems to fold over the shadow, above and below: you are seeing its far side, its light lensed over the top and under the bottom of the hole.

\Delta\phi = \dfrac{4GM}{c^{2}\,b} = \dfrac{2r_{s}}{b}
Einstein's deflection — twice Newton's value, as measured in the 1919 eclipse

Watching closely, you can catch the same patch of disk appear twice — once above, once below — the two images of one thing.

The Engine

\delta = \dfrac{\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}}{1-\beta\cos\theta} \qquad \dfrac{I_{\mathrm{obs}}}{I_{\mathrm{em}}} = \delta^{4}

Past the ISCO

Two Clocks

Your body feels nothing — free fall is free fall. But your wristwatch and the universe have stopped agreeing:

d\tau = dt\,\sqrt{1-\dfrac{r_{s}}{r}}
Gravitational time dilation — here, your second is 1.3 of theirs

Far above, mission control sees your image slow, redden, and dim. You see them speed up. Both of you are right — that is the whole trouble with time.

CHAPTER IV — TIME & TIDES · R = 1.5 rs

The Photon Sphere

You fall through the altitude where light itself orbits — some of the glow ahead of you has circled the hole more than once before reaching your eye. And gravity has begun to take you apart, feet first:

\Delta a_{\parallel} \approx \dfrac{2GM\ell}{r^{3}} \qquad \Delta a_{\perp} \approx -\dfrac{GM\ell}{r^{3}}
Tidal acceleration — ≈ 6×10⁶ g across a human body, here

Stellar-mass holes are the cruel ones: you'd be drawn out like smoke. A supermassive one would be gentle — at the horizon of Sagittarius A*, the tide across your body is one ten-thousandth of a g. Small holes tear you apart at the door; the giants swallow you whole.

The Horizon

The coefficient of time itself vanishes

The River

There is a coordinate system that makes the interior almost readable — space itself flows, like a river, toward the singularity:

ds^{2} = -(c\,dT)^{2} + \Big(dr + c\sqrt{\dfrac{r_{s}}{r}}\;dT\Big)^{2} + r^{2}d\Omega^{2}
Painlevé–Gullstrand — space flowing inward at c√(rₛ/r)

Outside, the river runs slower than light and you can swim out. At the horizon it matches c. In here it runs faster — and upstream is no longer a direction that exists. You cannot swim out of a river that flows faster than light.

The Inheritance

In 1992, Lee Smolin noticed something uncomfortable about our own universe: its constants are suspiciously good at making black holes. His heretical proposal — universes reproduce. Every horizon is a birth canal; every bounce, a child with slightly different physics. And the information, at least, seems to survive the trip:

S_{\mathrm{rad}}(t) \;=\; \min\!\Big(S_{\mathrm{thermal}},\; 2\,S_{\mathrm{BH}}\Big)
The Page curve — what falls in comes back out, eventually, scrambled beyond recognition

Two stories fit every measurement we have ever made. In one, r = 0 is a wall, and nothing continues. In the other, it is a door — and you just came through it. Physics cannot yet tell you which universe you are living in. But you can. You are in the one where you fell, and kept going.

CHAPTER ∞ — THE NEW PHYSICS

The Constants Are Not Yours

Look closely at the starlight. It is almost right. Almost. The fine structure constant — the number that fixes how tightly atoms hold their electrons, and therefore every color any star can ever emit — came through the door slightly changed:

\alpha = \dfrac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}\hbar c} \approx \dfrac{1}{137} \qquad \dfrac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \approx -2\,\dfrac{\Delta\alpha}{\alpha}
One part in ten thousand — and every spectral line in this sky shifts

This universe inherited its parent's equations, but not its numbers. The stars lean a shade your eyes almost recognize — a little too violet, a little too teal. Chemistry here will be subtly, irreversibly its own.
